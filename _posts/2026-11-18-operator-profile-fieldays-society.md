The New Zealand National Fieldays Society is the not-for-profit incorporated society, and registered charity, that owns the Fieldays agricultural event and the 114-hectare Mystery Creek Events Centre beside Hamilton Airport in the Waikato. It is worth a profile because it is the cleanest public case of a business built around one event: four days in June that bring more than 100,000 people to the edge of Hamilton and bring in nearly all of the society's money, and eleven months of trying to make a showground pay for itself.

## A farmers' week that outgrew a racecourse

The society says the idea came from a Waikato farmer who saw the Royal Agricultural Show in Britain in 1966 and wrote to the Waikato Times suggesting Hamilton try something similar. The society was formed in 1968, and the first event, a "Town and Country Fair", ran at Te Rapa Racecourse in 1969. The organisers expected a few thousand people; the society's own history says more than 10,000 came. In 1971 it bought the land at Mystery Creek Road, which is still the business's main asset.

Fieldays has since become the event the society describes as the Southern Hemisphere's largest agricultural show. Its 2019 edition had 128,747 visitors and 1,067 exhibitors across 1,559 sites, according to the society's annual report for that year. After Covid, the numbers came back more slowly: about 105,000 in June 2023, 110,000 in 2025, and more than 132,000 this June, which the society says was the first time in five years that gate numbers passed 130,000, with exhibitor sites sold out before the event.

## How the money works

The society's audited accounts for the year to 30 September 2025, filed on the Charities Register, are short and reconcile to the hundred dollars. They show a business with one product and a venue attached.

|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Fieldays | $9,241,500 | $9,662,500 | 88.9% |
| Other events at Mystery Creek | $959,600 | $1,023,100 | 9.2% |
| Property rental | $67,100 | $127,900 | 0.6% |
| Other, including grants of $31,000 | $124,000 | $105,300 | 1.2% |
| Interest | $2,100 | $0 | 0.0% |
| **Total** | **$10,394,300** | **$10,918,800** | **100%** |

Lines sum exactly to the stated totals in both years; shares are rounded.

Fieldays' own direct costs were $3,158,700, which leaves the event contributing about $6.08m. Almost all of that goes on running the organisation and the site the rest of the year: $3.35m of salaries, $1.61m of other overheads, $760,100 of property costs, $957,100 of depreciation and $225,000 of interest. The surplus for the year was $34,100, or 0.3 percent of revenue. The year before it was $537,900.

The cash cycle is the part most event businesses will recognise. Exhibitors pay for sites well ahead of June. The society's 2019 annual report said that front-loaded site money is what let it repay its bank each February. The 2025 balance sheet shows the other side of it: at 30 September the society held $1,400 of petty cash, $1,486,400 drawn on an overdraft, and $2,205,000 of term loans.

## What Covid cost, and what it still costs

Few businesses show the risk of depending on a single date as plainly. In the year to September 2022, when Covid restrictions pushed Fieldays out of its June slot, revenue fell to $1.45m and the society lost $3,964,800. The postponed event then landed in the same financial year as the June 2023 one, so 2023 carried two Fieldays, $16.9m of revenue and a $3.03m surplus. That made one good year out of two bad ones; it did not create anything new.

Equity is the honest scorecard. It was $27,875,600 at September 2019 and $21,633,800 at September 2022, a fall of $6.24m over the Covid years. By September 2025 it was back to $25,235,900, still $2.64m short of where it started. In October 2023 the society borrowed another $750,000 and raised its overdraft limit by $750,000, which its annual report described as moving historical losses out of the overdraft and into long-term debt. Its 2025 accounts note that no scholarships were awarded in 2024 or 2025 because the society is "focused on financial recovery".

## What is worth copying

**Own the date and the ground.** The society does not rent a venue for its main event; it owns one and rents it to others. The 2025 accounts carry $29.7m of property, plant and equipment, including land held at $8m. That is why a $3.96m loss in one year was survivable and why the bank keeps lending against a business with $1,400 in the till.

**Sell to exhibitors first and visitors second.** The 2025 fast facts say 70 percent of tickets were sold online and list the leads exhibitors collected through wristband taps. The real customer is the exhibitor, who pays before Christmas for a site in June. Any visitor attraction that can sell space or partnerships ahead of its season gets the same cash advantage.

**Measure what the event does for everyone else, and say so.** The society's 2019 annual report put Fieldays 2019 at $549m of sales for New Zealand businesses, $247m added to GDP and more than 2,000 jobs, and its 2023 report published a similar estimate by region. Those are the society's own commissioned figures and should be read as advocacy. But they are what an event uses to argue for public transport, roading and council support, and nearly 19,000 people used free buses to Mystery Creek this June, up 35 percent on 2025, by the society's count.

## Where the model runs out of road

**The event is not getting richer.** Fieldays revenue was $9,889,800 in 2019 and $9,241,500 in 2025, 6.6 percent less in dollars before any allowance for inflation, on about 15 percent fewer visitors. Over the same six years salaries rose 18 percent, from $2,825,800 to $3,345,600. A business that earns 89 percent of its income in four days needs those four days to grow at least as fast as its year-round costs, and up to 2025 they had not.

**Diversification has been subtraction as much as addition.** In 2019 the society also ran Equidays, an equestrian event that brought in $1,113,900 that year. It no longer appears in the accounts. Other events at Mystery Creek grew from $736,300 to $959,600, up 30 percent, but at 9 percent of revenue they cushion Fieldays rather than replace it. The 2019 surplus of $768,300 included a one-off $495,000 donation from the Gallagher Group; without it the year made about $273,000.

**The site needs more money than the business makes.** In October 2025 the society started a $3.2m upgrade of the water network across Mystery Creek. That is more than three years of 2025 operating cash flow ($1,011,200), against $1,472,000 of unused overdraft at balance date. The accounts do not say how it will be paid for. The board's going-concern note leans on the $25.2m of net assets and its confidence that borrowings can be refinanced, which is another way of saying the land is the backstop.

**Weather and the farm cycle set the gate.** The society's own releases credit 2026's crowd to a "buoyant rural sector" and four days of good weather. Neither is in its control. A year of low farm prices or a wet week in June lands straight on the one line that carries the business, and there is no second season to make it up.

The June 2026 event, with its sold-out sites and 20 percent more visitors, falls in the year to 30 September 2026, whose accounts are due by March 2027. That filing will show whether a strong year can rebuild the reserves, or whether it mostly pays for the pipes.

## What to take from it

If your business lives on one season or one event, Fieldays is a case for owning the asset the event sits on, selling the business side of the event before the public side, and keeping evidence of what you are worth to the region you operate in. It is also a warning. A single date makes a business only as strong as its worst June, and a venue that costs money all year needs either an event that grows faster than wages or a calendar that genuinely fills. Six years of audited accounts show the society has not yet found either, and that its reserves have carried the difference.
