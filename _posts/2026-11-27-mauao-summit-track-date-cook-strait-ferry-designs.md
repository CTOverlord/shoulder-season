It's Friday, and the news is about getting things back: a mountain, a ferry service people can trust, and a little order at the boarding gate. Six items.

## Mauao's summit track could reopen on 20 December, if iwi and the Mauao trust agree

A Tauranga council report has put a possible date of 20 December on reopening the summit track on Mauao, the mountain at Mount Maunganui that has been closed since the landslide at Beachside Holiday Park killed six people on 22 January. The council has spent more than $7 million on its initial response, expert advice and the works needed to reopen it. Councillors meet on 1 December to consider the report.

The reopening would be partial. The report says the boardwalk, staircase and track work needs fine weather to be finished by the 20th, many other tracks will stay closed with no timeframe, and more fencing, security cameras (a six-week job) and a possible sign-in, sign-out system are planned. Any reopening also needs the agreement of iwi and the Mauao trust. Mt Maunganui Ratepayers Association president Michael O'Neill told RNZ the business community would welcome an opening before Christmas.

For Mount Maunganui the summit walk is the free attraction that fills the cafes, the parking and the beachfront accommodation. Losing it for a full summer and most of a year has been the quiet cost underneath a much larger tragedy, and the government inquiry into the landslides is not due to report until February.

## The 2029 Cook Strait ferries get their detailed design, and Picton's port work stays on schedule

Ferry Holdings, KiwiRail and Guangzhou Shipyard International have released the detailed designs for the two hybrid ferries that will replace the Interislander fleet in 2029. The ships are larger than the current ferries, carry more vehicles and passengers, and are expected to emit 30 percent less carbon for every passenger and unit of cargo. Ferry Holdings chair Chris Mackenzie pointed to extra redundancy in propulsion, steering and power, so that a system failure can be recovered in minutes, which matters on a route that spends much of its time in the Marlborough Sounds and Wellington Harbour. A lower-wake hull, solar panels and battery storage are also in the design.

The port work is the part that dates the transition. In Picton a transition berth for Interislander is due in March 2027, with construction of the new wharf expected to start in February 2027. In Wellington, removal of the old infrastructure is due to finish in March 2027, and work on the wharf extension began in July. Wellington harbourmaster Grant Nalder told RNZ he was "quietly optimistic", but noted the strait still lacks a quickly available ocean-going tug. Marlborough mayor Nadine Taylor called the ships the district's "2029 Christmas present", and said the old ferries' reliability had improved in the meantime. For anyone selling a self-drive or rail itinerary across the strait, that is three more peak seasons on the current ships.

## Flight Centre investigates a breach of the accommodation system used by Travel Associates and Cruiseabout

Flight Centre told the Australian Securities Exchange it is investigating unusual activity in a third-party accommodation booking system used by some of its leisure brands, and that a significant number of customer records may have been accessed. The activity has so far been found only in the system used by Travel Associates, Cruiseabout and some independent agents. Flight bookings and corporate travel are not affected. Most of the accessed records are names, some with basic travel details; a very small share includes limited passport details, contact details, birth dates or, rarely, health or mobility information. No payment data has been found to be accessed, and Mandiant is helping with the response.

It is the second warning in a week about booking data in the travel chain: on Monday we covered scams built on breached booking-site details that already know the traveller's name and dates. The practical point for operators and accommodation is the same one Flight Centre gave its own customers. Expect guests to receive convincing messages about their bookings over the holidays, and make it easy for them to check with you directly.

## Air New Zealand starts weighing carry-on bags at the gate, and Jetstar will charge for locker space from February

Air New Zealand says it will enforce its cabin baggage rules strictly through the summer peak, with more scales and payment terminals at boarding gates. The economy allowance stays at one 7kg bag of up to 55 by 40 by 23cm, plus a small personal item. A bag that fails the check goes in the hold at $60 on a domestic flight, $115 to Australia and most of the Pacific, and $195 elsewhere. Chief operations officer Kate Boyer said excess cabin baggage is the fifth-largest cause of delays at five to six minutes per affected flight. Cirium data cited by RNZ put the airline's October on-time arrival rate at 86.7 percent, 12 points up on a year earlier.

Jetstar goes further from 2 February: bookings will include one underseat bag, and overhead locker space becomes a paid "Priority Carry-On" extra from $23, with the 7kg weight limit dropped in favour of size limits. For operators who meet guests off domestic flights, a $60 surprise at the gate is now a real possibility to mention in pre-travel notes.

## New Plymouth backs an $8.9 million Brooklands Zoo upgrade, with fewer species and better enclosures

New Plymouth District Council voted 9-4 to go ahead with stage one of an $8.9 million upgrade of Brooklands Zoo in Pukekura Park, after a motion to pause it for the 2027-2037 long-term plan failed 6-8. The free zoo draws about 140,000 visitors a year and costs about $870,000 a year to run. The money covers a new otter enclosure at about $1.7 million, an aviary at $3.1 million and primate areas at $3.6 million, with work starting next year. Afterwards the zoo will hold 21 species instead of 29, with about eight fewer habitats.

The argument in the chamber was the one every free council attraction eventually has. Councillor Gordon Brown noted the zoo earned $900 of external revenue in a year. Mayor Max Brough, who voted to pause and then to proceed, said the zoo "brings people to town" and promised to push the budget down. Fewer, better exhibits is the direction zoos everywhere are taking; New Plymouth has now committed to paying for it.

## The biggest gathering of voyaging waka in centuries opens to the public in Doubtless Bay tomorrow

Te Hau Kōmaru, the biennial waka hourua festival, has spent the week at Aurere in Northland's Doubtless Bay, its first time in Northland. Six waka from around Aotearoa are there, along with two double-hulled voyaging canoes that sailed from Hawaiʻi and one from Vanuatu, and voyagers from Tahiti, Samoa and Rarotonga. Coordinator Joyce Munns Conrad expected more than 2,000 people across the week, about 1,000 of them Far North school children. Aurere is where the late Sir Hekenukumai Busby built the Kupe Waka Centre and the voyaging canoe Te Aurere.

The festival closes on Saturday 28 November with a free public open day from 9am to 1pm at Aurere, Taipā and Mangōnui, with workshops at all three sites and short sailing trips from the Mangōnui waterfront on a first-come basis. Events like this are the kind of cultural product Northland is often told to build, arriving on its own terms through the voyaging community rather than through a visitor strategy.
