## A coroner says social media is leading unprepared visitors into alpine danger near Brewster Glacier

Coroner Alexandra Cunninghame released her findings today into the death of Maëlle Favregros, a 23-year-old French visitor who died from blood loss after a fall on slippery, rocky ground near the Brewster Glacier in Mount Aspiring National Park in February last year. She was not on a managed track, was not wearing tramping boots, and had found the trip on AllTrails. The coroner urged trampers to plan from official sources such as DOC and Plan My Walk, stay on marked routes, and carry the skills and gear alpine terrain needs.

The Brewster Track leaves the Haast Pass highway and climbs about 1,000 metres in roughly 2.8 kilometres. Mountain Safety Council chief executive Mike Daisley told RNZ the area was quiet until about six years ago, when visits spiked. His advice was to treat social media as "inspiration, but don't consider that your plan". DOC has since put new warning signs at the carpark and at Brewster Hut, run targeted safety advertising on social media, and worked with AllTrails so the app shows the hazards and skills involved. An interagency group has been working on the area since April 2024.

For anyone selling the outdoors, the uncomfortable part is that the channel doing the selling is the same one that strips out the context. A photo of the glacier travels; the warning about what lies past the end of the track does not. Operators and visitor centres in the Haast and Wānaka corridor are now part of the correction, whether they asked to be or not.

## Rockpool raiders face $250 fines on the Auckland and Coromandel coasts from today

From today, fishery officers can issue $250 infringement notices to people breaking the indefinite ban on taking some sea life from parts of the Auckland and Coromandel coastlines. The ban came into force this month after a review. Cockles, crabs, kina and pīpī are excluded from it, but daily limits still apply in the open areas. Oceans and Fisheries Minister Shane Jones said officers would be "out in force over summer", and the fine gives them a middle option between a quiet word and a prosecution. Campaigners told 1News the fines are a start but more rockpool closures are needed.

This matters to anyone hosting summer visitors on those coasts, from holiday parks to bach rentals. The ban is new, the species list has exceptions, and the people most likely to get it wrong are the ones on holiday: domestic families who have gathered shellfish the same way for years, and international visitors who have never heard of the rules. A line in the welcome notes will save someone a $250 surprise.

## Southern Cross says Kiwis are planning dearer domestic holidays but rarely insuring them

Southern Cross Travel Insurance says New Zealanders are taking more expensive trips at home but are much less likely to insure them than an overseas trip. It has paid more than $965,000 on domestic holiday claims since July 2024, for things like rental vehicle excess, disrupted travel and lost non-refundable bookings. Of its 833 domestic claims, only 34 were for $5,000 or more, but they made up more than a quarter of the total paid. The largest single payout was $13,890, for a trip that never started.

The detail is the useful part. Changing a trip before departure made up 28 percent of claims but 44 percent of dollars paid, averaging $1,831 a claim. Claimants over 70 averaged $2,628 against $754 for those under 30, and almost a third of claims involved a flight. Read from the supply side, this is a reminder that domestic guests now spend like international ones on Great Walks, lodges and campervan loops, and carry the same exposure when weather or a cancelled flight intervenes. Cancellation terms that are clear at the point of booking do more for a guest than any apology after the fact.

## Kaikōura borrows another $432,000 to keep its flood repairs moving while it waits on NZTA

Kaikōura District Council has agreed to borrow an extra $432,306 on top of $2 million already approved, about $2.4 million in all, to keep flood recovery going after more than 450mm of rain hit the district on 7 and 8 July. It has already spent $793,602. First on the list is reopening the sealed road and protection work at the Cribb Creek bridge on Kaikōura Inland Road, which the flood washed away. The council has applied to NZTA for emergency works funding, which chief executive Will Doughty expects to cover about 71 percent.

The council gave NZTA three options: $8.36 million for basic repairs, $11.74 million for a mid-range recovery, or $29.07 million for long-term resilience. Indications are that the basic option will be chosen, leaving Kaikōura's share at about $2.4 million spread across just 3,000 ratepayers, with a rates cap coming. Environment Canterbury has spent $2.4 million on its own repairs, approved another $5.09 million in debt, and faces an estimated bill of up to $33 million over several years. Kaikōura's visitor economy pays for these roads twice: once when they close, and again when a small district has to choose the least costly way to reopen them.

## A Haast Pass fibre cable gives researchers the closest look yet inside the Alpine Fault

Researchers from New Zealand, Australia, Singapore and China turned a Chorus fibre optic cable through the Haast Pass into an earthquake sensor for five months in 2023, firing nearly a thousand pulses of light down it every second. Victoria University professor John Townend, whose team released the study this week, told RNZ the fault is not a single clean crack but a band of damaged rock about 200 metres wide, with six narrower zones of crushed rock inside it. The most prominent runs for about 180 kilometres and is probably where recent large earthquakes slipped.

The Alpine Fault has produced a major quake about every 260 years on average, and the last was around 1717. Townend was careful to say it is "not like clockwork". For the visitor economy the West Coast and the passes across the Alps are the exposure: roads that already close for slips and floods would be the first casualties of a big rupture, and better knowledge of where the fault is likely to stop slipping feeds directly into how the region plans for it.
