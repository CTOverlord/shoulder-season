It's Thursday, and the view from the Queenstown waterfront this week has been smoke. Five items. Here's what's moving.

## A controlled burn reignites and 1,400 hectares burn above Lake Whakatipu

A vegetation fire that broke out at Walter Peak Station on Tuesday had burnt an estimated 1,400 hectares of tussock, scrub and some trees by Wednesday night, according to Fire and Emergency NZ. It has spread from the station along the McKinlays Creek area and into parts of the adjoining conservation estate and Cecil Peak Station. Five helicopters with monsoon buckets worked the most active edges on Wednesday, with 23 firefighters and Department of Conservation crews on the ground, and the same strength was planned for Thursday. No homes or buildings have been damaged.

The station is owned by RealNZ, which runs the Walter Peak visitor experience reached by the TSS Earnslaw. Chief executive Dave Beeche said the company carried out a controlled burn last Wednesday with Fire and Emergency on site, and that the fire service believes it somehow reignited; RealNZ will work with investigators on how. On Tuesday the fire came within 300 metres of the historic homestead and 350 guests and staff were evacuated. Tours were cancelled, and RealNZ said it hoped to resume them on Thursday, barring changes overnight.

The wider lesson is the date. Spring burns to renew high-country pasture are routine around the Wakatipu at this time of year, as Mayor John Glover pointed out, and this one was planned and supervised. It still got away in a dry, windy week in October, before the summer fire season has properly started. Boats have been asked to stay 500 metres offshore while helicopters work, and smoke will drift across the basin as the wind shifts.

## The Otago Central Lakes Deal is signed, and the question of who pays for visitors is parked until after the election

The government signed its City and Regional Deal with Queenstown Lakes, Central Otago and the Otago Regional Council in Queenstown on Tuesday: a 10-year partnership under a 30-year vision that the ministers' release describes as harnessing the region's growth as the country's premier tourism destination. It covers housing, transport, health, electricity transmission and diversification into film and technology, with an implementation plan due within six months. It follows deals with Auckland and the Western Bay of Plenty, and Infrastructure Minister Chris Bishop says it holds whatever the election result.

What it does not settle is the money for visitor-driven infrastructure. The deal originally carried a commitment to explore an accommodation levy in 2027, and talks stalled in August when National ruled out a bed tax. What was signed is, in Bishop's words, a commitment to work together on "getting that funding flow". Mayor John Glover said it was unreasonable to expect agreed wording before the election and that the councils want the Crown to acknowledge the cost tourism puts on them.

Released the same day, NZTA's 30-year Queenstown Strategic Network Plan projects peak-day population rising from about 83,000 in 2025 to about 143,000 by 2055. Early priorities are the Frankton Flats intersection, bus priority, Ladies Mile and the southern corridor, a better town centre bus hub, and replacing the Edith Cavell Bridge at Arthurs Point. Gondolas or cable cars are named as a longer-term option, with private proposals under discussion, alongside a second Kawarau River crossing. Bishop was candid that it is not a funding plan.

## Waitangi heads a new World Heritage shortlist that leans heavily on cultural sites

The Department of Conservation has named nine sites for New Zealand's revised World Heritage Tentative List, the step before a UNESCO nomination. They are the Waitangi Treaty Grounds with Te Tii Marae; Mataatua Wharenui in Whakatāne; Kate Sheppard House in Christchurch; Parihaka Papakāinga in Taranaki; Te Kauwhanganui Māori Parliament building and collection at Morrinsville; Te Hauturu o Toi/Little Barrier Island; a Moriori cultural landscape on the Chatham Islands; Ngā Tapuwae o Mataaho, the Auckland volcanic field; and the Kermadec Islands and Marine Reserve.

The tilt is the story. New Zealand's three existing World Heritage sites, Tongariro, Te Wāhipounamu and the Subantarctic Islands, are all recognised for natural values, with Tongariro also inscribed for its cultural significance. At least six of the nine new candidates are put forward for cultural values, and the Waitangi National Trust says the Treaty Grounds would be the country's first site recognised for cultural heritage alone. Nothing is quick: a site must sit on the tentative list for at least a year, and DOC expects nominators to be ready with a full dossier within ten years. The Trust is explicit about the visitor case, arguing that inscription would draw cultural travellers to Waitangi and the wider North.

## Spending falls for a second month, and Marlborough and the West Coast feel it most

Core retail spending through Paymark's network was $3.57 billion in September, down 1.3 percent on a year earlier, after a 1.8 percent fall in August. Paymark's core retail measure includes hotels, bars, cafés and restaurants along with shops. Chief sales officer Bruce Proffit blamed petrol prices that jumped in mid-September, interest rates that have risen over three months, and a cold, wet last weekend of the month. He said the country was off to a slower start than usual towards the pre-Christmas surge.

The regional table matters for visitor towns. Marlborough was weakest, down 9.7 percent on a year ago, followed by the West Coast at 3.3 percent down, Wellington and the Bay of Plenty. Gisborne and Hawke's Bay grew. With domestic travellers carrying much of the shoulder season, a household that is paying more at the pump and the bank is a household that takes the shorter trip, which is the backdrop to Tuesday's hospitality liquidation numbers.

## Health NZ is owed more than $70 million by patients not entitled to free care

Health New Zealand says tourists and migrants who are not eligible for publicly funded care owe it more than $70 million, up from $45.6 million in 2022/23 and $18 million across the former district health boards in 2019/20. Chief financial officer Bevan McKenzie put part of the rise down to travel growing again since the pandemic, and said 25 to 30 percent of outstanding debt is typically written off.

Health NZ has not said how much of the total is owed by short-stay visitors rather than migrants, and most of the cases RNZ obtained involve people on medical-treatment visas. The point for the visitor economy is narrower: most people on visas of under 12 months are not entitled to public healthcare, yet travel insurance is a visa condition only for some categories, such as international students and parents, not for an ordinary visitor visa. Treatment is given regardless of ability to pay, and the debt follows the patient home.
