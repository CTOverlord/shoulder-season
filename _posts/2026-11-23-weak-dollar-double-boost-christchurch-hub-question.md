It's Monday, and a quiet weekend leaves the exchange rate doing most of the talking. Six items.

## ASB says the weak dollar will stay weak, and that tourism is one of the winners

The New Zealand dollar is trading at about US55c, against a 20-year average of roughly US70c and a range of 50c to 88c over that period, ASB senior economist Chris Tennent-Brown told RNZ. It is historically low against the euro, the pound and the Australian dollar too; the Japanese yen is the one major currency that is weaker still. His explanation is interest rates. The official cash rate is low by historical standards, the Reserve Bank of Australia's is a couple of percentage points higher, and markets expect the US to lift further. ASB expects the kiwi to stay below US60c "over the coming quarters" for as long as New Zealand's rates stay low.

For tourism he described it as a double boost over summer: New Zealand is cheaper for international visitors, and more expensive for New Zealanders thinking of going abroad, which nudges some of them into holidaying at home. He called it a "two-speed economy", with exporters, tourism operators and offshore investors on the right side of it and households buying imports on the wrong side.

The catch sits in the same sentence. Diesel, petrol and jet fuel are priced in US dollars, so the weak currency compounds fuel costs that were already high, and that lands in airfares and in the cost of moving people around the country. An operator pricing in New Zealand dollars for a visitor paying in euros or US dollars has room to move; an operator whose biggest input cost is fuel has less of it than the headline suggests.

## Air New Zealand is back in Christchurch long-haul, and an aviation academic asks whether the regions will feel it

Massey University aviation management lecturer Ajai Jayathilakan, in an analysis published by RNZ, sets out the scale of the shift. Air New Zealand's Christchurch to Singapore 787 service started on 28 October, Tokyo Narita follows on Saturday and Perth on 30 November, with a seasonal Bali service planned for winter 2027. He writes that the Singapore route will run year-round. Christchurch Airport is heading into its biggest international summer, with international seat capacity up 21 percent and long-haul capacity up 54 percent.

His argument is that the airline's advantage over the foreign carriers already flying in is control of the domestic network. Running its own international aircraft lets Air New Zealand time arrivals to connect with regional flights to Nelson, Queenstown, Dunedin, Hokitika or Invercargill, so a visitor from Tokyo need never see Auckland. Whether that happens is another matter. He cites NZ Airports figures for the first half of 2026: on routes where Jetstar competes, Air New Zealand's fares fell 4 percent and capacity rose 0.6 percent; on routes without Jetstar, fares rose 4.2 percent and seats fell by the same proportion.

His suggestions are coordinated international and regional schedules with through-ticketing and baggage transfer, support for the regional airports around Christchurch, the monitoring framework NZ Airports has already proposed, and a public service obligation model for routes that cannot pay their way. For South Island operators outside Christchurch, the useful test this summer is simple: whether a guest landing from Singapore or Tokyo can reach them the same day at a fare they will pay.

## Booking-site breaches are feeding scams that know the traveller's name and dates

Bookit, Booking.com and, most recently, Expedia have each confirmed data breaches in recent months, and the Travel Agents' Association says the stolen data is behind a run of convincing phishing attempts. Chief executive Julie White told RNZ the messages often know the traveller's name and travel dates, and the common request is to "update your card details" because of a system error. University of Auckland commercial law professor Alex Sims added that AI-built websites are now close copies of real accommodation providers, so a guest can book what they believe is a real hotel through a fake site.

White's advice to travellers was to book with New Zealand entities, because New Zealand consumer law does not reach an offshore third party. For accommodation and activity operators the practical exposure is different: guests who book through a platform and then receive a fraudulent message in the operator's name will often contact the operator, not the platform. Telling guests up front how and when the business will ask for payment, and that it will never ask for card details by message, is cheap insurance.

## Rotorua opens consultation today on alcohol licence fees that could rise 140 percent

Rotorua Lakes Council voted last Wednesday, unanimously with one abstention, to consult on an Alcohol Fees Bylaw 2027. New legislation lets councils set their own licensing fees instead of the national fees, which have not changed since 2013. The council recovered just under 56 percent of its licensing costs through fees in 2024/25, leaving ratepayers to cover $274,000, and wants licence holders to pay about 85 percent. Application and annual fees for many premises could rise by as much as 140 percent, and special licence fees for events by about 48 percent. In the highest-risk category, one fee goes from $1,437.50 to $3,453.

Reg Hennessy, who owns Hennessy's Irish Bar and is president of Hospitality New Zealand's Rotorua branch, called the numbers "mind-boggling", said the costs would be passed to customers, and said the industry would challenge the proposal "right to the end". An economic assessment of the change has not been done; chief executive Andrew Moraes said that was the kind of feedback the council wanted. Consultation runs from today, with a decision early next year.

This is the local end of the argument covered on Friday in Wellington, where Hospitality NZ backed a single national fee system. Every council that adopts its own bylaw adds another line to the evening cost of a visitor's night out, and Rotorua's numbers are the first big ones on the table.

## A Coromandel bridge budgeted at $12 million in 2016 will now cost $65 million

The replacement Kirikiri Stream Bridge on State Highway 26, with the nearby SH26/SH25A intersection, was funded at $12 million in 2016. Budget 2026 has put $65 million against it, a rise of more than 440 percent, Local Democracy Reporting found. NZTA says the earlier estimate predates Covid and the jump in labour, steel and material costs; a 2023 tender came in at about $35 million, with total funding needs of $47 million. The bridge and parts of SH26 will be raised three to four metres to clear floodwater and debris. NZTA says it is now reviewing the scope for the best value within the available funding.

The rest of the $85 million Coromandel allocation funds work at the Manaia River and Owera bridges between March and July 2027, with more sites at Whenuakite, Waikawau, Te Kouma Hill and Whangapoua Hill staged over four years. Separate Crown funding covers nine sites over the same March to July window, including underslip and rockfall work on the SH25 Thames Coast Road and underslip treatment on SH2 in the Karangahake Gorge. Thames-Coromandel mayor Peter Revell has said visitors need confidence they can get on and off the peninsula. The work will help that over time, but it lands in the autumn shoulder of 2027, so operators there should plan for roadworks then.

## Kaikōura adopts an economic plan to keep its young people, with no budget behind it yet

Kaikōura District Council adopted its economic development strategy and action plan on 11 November after two years of work. Projections show the over-65 population growing by more than 40 percent over the next ten years to a third of residents, while the working-age population could shrink. The plan has 26 actions over five years, to be delivered with Te Rūnanga o Kaikōura, Enterprise North Canterbury, Destination Kaikōura, businesses and government agencies. Astrotourism, The Whale Trail and the Wakatu Quay development are named as sources of jobs.

Corporate services senior manager Peter Kearney was candid about the constraint. The council has no economic development role, the work falls to him without funding, and he will ask for "a small amount" in next year's Long Term Plan from a ratepayer base of around 3,000 properties. A town whose economy rests on visitors is betting on more visitor products to hold its workforce, which is a sound bet only if the people to staff them can afford to live there; housing is the first of the plan's four pillars for that reason.
