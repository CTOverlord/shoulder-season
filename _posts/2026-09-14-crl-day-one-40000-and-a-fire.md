It's Monday, and the weekend's big event was a railway opening, which gets its real test during this morning's rush hour. Here's what's moving.

## The City Rail Link opened on Sunday, with a fire an hour in and service mostly back by lunchtime

The first passenger train through Auckland's City Rail Link left Ōtāhuhu at 4.57am on Sunday, with more than 400 people queued before dawn to catch it. At about 5.50am an electrical fire in a lighting cable above a platform at Waitematā Station forced an evacuation, and 1News reports Te Waihorotiu was cleared too. Fire and Emergency put it out quickly. There were delays and cancellations on every line, the South-City and East-West lines were back to their normal 15-minute frequency by 9am, and RNZ reports service had mostly recovered by noon.

Auckland Transport chief executive Stacey van der Putten's line was "It's an active railway", and on the numbers the crowds were not put off. Auckland Transport gave two figures during the day, and they measure different things, so they should not be added together or compared:

- About 14,300 people had travelled across the whole rail network by lunchtime, as reported by RNZ at 12.56pm.
- Nearly 40,000 people had used the new underground stations by 3pm, as reported by 1News.

A Sunday crowd that came to ride the tunnel is not a commute, and this morning's timetable is the first one that matters. It is the transitional timetable: six trains an hour at peak at most suburban stations rather than the eight that were tested, and gaps of up to 15 minutes off-peak.

For anyone moving visitors around central Auckland, the fire is the useful lesson. Waitematā, the old Britomart, is the station nearest the cruise wharves and the downtown hotels, and the cruise season has been running since Crown Princess arrived on 29 August. A single fault there on Sunday caused delays on every line. For the next few weeks, give guests who have a ship or a transfer to catch a buffer, and a backup plan that does not rely on the train.

## DOC and the Backcountry Trust will look at new ways of paying for huts, and have committed to nothing yet

Conservation Minister Tama Potaka announced on Friday that the Department of Conservation and the Backcountry Trust have signed a memorandum of understanding covering the backcountry hut and track network. The two organisations plan to write a national operational agreement and aim to sign it by the end of 2026.

Read the release for what the memorandum does not do, because that list is longer than what it does. It is non-binding. It creates no legal or operational obligations, transfers none of DOC's responsibilities, decides nothing about any individual hut or track, commits neither side to a future arrangement, and changes nothing about how anything is managed today.

What it does commit to exploring is the line worth watching: new funding and visitor payment systems for the network. DOC looks after nearly 1,000 huts and 24,000 kilometres of track. The trust has spent close to ten years restoring huts such as Almer and Hopeless and hunting bases such as Venison Tops, and has had $4.2 million from the international visitor levy since 2024. A volunteer trust is not in a position to charge anyone for anything, so any "visitor payment system" that comes out of this would be DOC's to run. For guided walking and hunting operators who use backcountry huts, the end-of-year agreement is the document to read, and it is the one that could change what a night in a hut costs.

## Kaikōura gets $835,000 of visitor levy money, aimed at the people who arrive by cruise ship

Tourism and Hospitality Minister Louise Upston announced $835,000 from the International Visitor Levy for Kaikōura on Saturday. The release describes three uses: better facilities and arrival experiences for cruise visitors, improved public spaces used by visitors and locals, and a more welcoming gateway into the township. It names no individual projects and gives no breakdown.

The reasoning in the release is the part that applies elsewhere. Upston described Kaikōura as having "a small ratepayer base but high visitor numbers", which is exactly the problem the levy was sold as fixing. To put the grant against that ratepayer base:

- Grant: $835,000
- Kaikōura District residents at the 2023 Census: about 4,200
- Grant per resident: $835,000 divided by 4,200, or roughly $200 each

That is the kind of money a council of that size could not easily raise itself for facilities that mostly serve people who do not live there. The release is silent on how many cruise calls or passengers the upgrades are for, so it cannot be tested against a per-visitor figure yet. That is the number to ask for when the projects are named.

## NZ First wants to take automatic work rights off international students

NZ First leader Winston Peters announced 14 immigration policies at the annual Immigration Conference in Auckland on Friday. The headline items are a referendum on capping resident visas at 20,000 a year, the qualifying period for permanent residence going from two years to five, and the qualifying period for citizenship doubling from five years to ten.

For hospitality and tourism employers, the relevant item is further down the list. International students would lose their automatic work rights, have to apply under strict conditions, and could only be employed where they do not displace New Zealanders. They would also have to leave the country and apply from overseas to switch to a different visa. The party's summary does not say whether that covers part-time work during study, post-study work, or both, and RNZ describes it in terms of work after study. Until that is clarified, nobody can say how many existing staff it would touch.

The other half of the operator read is what the list leaves out. None of the 14 policies mention working holiday visas, seasonal workers, employer-sponsored work visas or visitor visas. National's Erica Stanford responded by asking who would be turned away, noting that close to half of primary resident applications are partnership visas and a similar share fill skills shortages. This is a platform for the 7 November election from a party currently in government, not a change to the rules.
