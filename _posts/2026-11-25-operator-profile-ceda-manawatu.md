Central Economic Development Agency Limited, known as CEDA, is the company Palmerston North City Council and Manawatū District Council own half each to promote Manawatū. It is the region's economic development agency and its regional tourism organisation in one. It is worth a profile because nearly every region funds something like it, few readers in tourism ever see how one is paid for, and CEDA's audited annual report for the year to 30 June 2026 sets that out in unusual detail.

## Two councils, one company

CEDA was incorporated on 15 October 2015 under the Companies Act. According to its 2017-18 statement of intent, it bought the region's two existing agencies, Vision Manawatū and Destination Manawatū, with effect from 1 September 2016, and spent its first year merging them. It is a council-controlled organisation under the Local Government Act, so it publishes a statement of intent each year, reports against it every six months and is audited by Grant Thornton on behalf of the Auditor-General.

Tourism is one strand of a wide brief. The same small team chases inward investment, works on Te Utanganui (the central New Zealand distribution hub planned around KiwiRail's freight hub at Bunnythorpe), runs the government's Regional Business Partner programme locally, works on talent attraction and the regional food strategy, and runs ManawatuNZ.co.nz, the region's visitor website. The board had six directors at 30 June, appointed by the two councils.

The published 2026 subtotals differ by $1 from the sum of their lines, which is rounding in the statements.

Palmerston North pays $2,093,040 of the core council funding and Manawatū District $708,822, which is roughly in proportion to their populations of 90,500 and 34,000. Counting another $66,195 of council money paid for specific projects, the councils supplied 77 percent of everything CEDA received, central government 19 percent and industry 3 percent, by CEDA's own breakdown.

The spending side is mostly people. Employee costs were $1,657,197, 46 percent of total costs of $3,595,563. CEDA's own allocation of those costs puts $961,037 into business retention and expansion (which includes the Regional Business Partner programme), $849,061 into sector development (which is where most visitor sector work sits), $636,807 into inward investment, $468,811 into marketing, $320,315 into partnerships, $182,747 into talent and skills and $176,786 into governance. Those seven figures add to the audited cost total within a dollar.

The result was a surplus of $144,012, after $118,874 the year before. Five years earlier, in the year to June 2021, CEDA ran a deficit of $183,678. It ended June 2026 with $1,246,141 in cash, no borrowings, and equity of $892,616.

## What the tourism work looks like

Tourism has no line of its own in those accounts, which says something in itself. The annual report describes the visitor work as implementing a destination management plan for the region, and lists what was done: a second year of the Manawatū Garden Festival (20 gardens, two events, 1,604 tickets sold), a "Visitor Collective" capability programme helping local operators build trade pitch decks, a new Allied Membership of the Tourism Export Council, a second year at TRENZ, the Regional Tourism New Zealand inbound operator event, and a first appearance at the Tourism Regional Showcase in Sydney. It also lists early work on projects in the plan's pipeline, among them a Gorge Quarter around Te Āpiti, the Manawatū Gorge; Tōtara Reserve; and a City to Sea rail trail.

The more interesting work sits under inward investment. CEDA keeps a hotel investment pitch deck, shares the region's commercial accommodation data with developers, and reports a confirmed opportunity for a hotel in central Palmerston North with an identified developer and management company, which it has taken to the city council. Most regional tourism organisations market beds that already exist. This one is also trying to add them.

## What is worth copying

**Put the visitor and investment pitches in one building.** A region short of hotel rooms cannot market its way to more overnight visitors, and CEDA's structure lets the people who know the occupancy numbers be the people who brief developers. That is a design choice any region could make, and most separate the two jobs.

**Pool two councils' money into one company.** A city and its surrounding district are one visitor market, and visitors do not notice the boundary. A jointly owned company avoids two half-funded agencies selling the same gorge.

**Fix the books before asking for more.** Turning a $183,678 deficit into two consecutive surpluses, on council funding that rose only 11 percent over five years ($288,390 in dollars), is a quieter achievement than a campaign, and it matters more to the councils deciding whether to keep paying.

**Say plainly what you do not control.** The report prints the regional economic indicators alongside a statement that CEDA does not measure its own performance against them, because exchange rates, natural disasters and government policy move them more than any agency can. That is honest, and it is the right starting point.

## Where the model runs out of road

That honesty is also the problem. CEDA's performance measures count activity: deliver nine sector development initiatives and report fifteen; deliver fifteen inward investment activities and report nineteen. They do not say whether a single extra visitor came. A ratepayer reading the report can see what CEDA did but not what it changed, and the agency's own design means it will never be blamed for a bad year or credited with a good one.

The regional visitor numbers did not move much. CEDA's report puts tourism card spending in Manawatū at $309m for the year to June 2026, down 0.4 percent, with domestic spending down $6.8m and international up $5.6m. It ranks the region 14th of 31 regional tourism organisations for visitor spend; its 2021 and 2025 reports both said 13th. And the headline series itself has shifted underneath the reader: the 2025 report gave $340m for the previous year, while the 2026 figure and its stated fall imply about $310m for that same year on the new basis, a change the footnote attributes to new monthly regional tourism estimates. Nobody can follow the trend across the two reports without doing that arithmetic.

The central government money is project money. Other services revenue came in at $761,797 against a budget of $147,000, mostly because Regional Business Partner funding arrived that had not been budgeted. Money that arrives unbudgeted can leave the same way, and it pays for staff.

Governance costs a lot at this size. Directors' fees were $167,500, 4.5 percent of total income, for a company spending $3.6m. Larger regional tourism organisations spread a board across a much bigger budget.

And three-quarters of the income is rates, the most contested money in local government. CEDA is funded because two councils choose to fund it every year, and the case it can make to them rests on activity counts and a regional indicator it says it does not answer for.

## What to take from it

If you run a regional tourism organisation or the economic development agency that contains one, CEDA is a good case for combining destination marketing with investment attraction, pooling neighbouring councils into one budget, and keeping the books in surplus. It is also a warning. An agency that measures only its own outputs is safe in a bad year and invisible in a good one, and when council budgets tighten, invisible is the more dangerous position. The fix is not to promise outcomes it cannot control, but to pick one or two it can genuinely move, such as overnight stays from the events it funds or hotel rooms its investment work helps open, and report them every year on a series that does not change.
