## Otago starts a two-year countdown to the 2028 total eclipse

Staff at Tūhura Otago Museum marked a milestone this week that has nothing to do with the current season and everything to do with planning: two years out from the total solar eclipse that will cross the lower South Island on 22 July 2028. The path of totality runs from Milford Sound through Central Otago to Ōtepoti Dunedin, with roughly three minutes of full cover around 4.15pm.

The reason it matters now rather than in 2028 is that the beds are already going. Museum director and astronomer Dr Ian Griffin, who has chased eclipses around the world, expects Otago to be one of the few places on the planet in totality that day and reckons anywhere from 20,000 to 50,000 visitors could head south to see it. For scale, a 2012 total eclipse in Queensland was credited with around A$20 million for its local economy. A winter Saturday afternoon in the middle of the school holidays is close to the worst possible time to move that many people through Central Otago's roads and accommodation, which is exactly why the operators paying attention are blocking out the date now.

## Dunedin bucks the cruise slump the rest of the coast is feeling

While the national cruise picture keeps sliding, Port Chalmers is heading the other way. Dunedin is set to be New Zealand's busiest cruise port next season, with vessel visits rising from 69 in 2025-26 to 85 in 2026-27, and 43 of those ships large enough to carry at least 2,000 passengers. The Dunedin City Council is trimming its extra cruise-day bus trips from 255 to 159, targeting the additional services at 28 of the 43 big ships rather than running them across the board.

The contrast with the rest of the country is the story. Cruise deployment to New Zealand has fallen roughly 40% since the 2023-24 peak, with lines citing rising port, council and government costs and regulatory changes made after itineraries were already sold. Napier is down to 50 booked calls for 2026-27 from a record 89 two years earlier, the Bay of Islands is bracing for a sharp drop, and Disney has pulled out of the country. Dunedin growing against that tide is worth understanding rather than assuming, and it is a reminder that "the cruise sector" is really a set of very different regional outcomes.

## The visitor levy is buying toilets in the busiest hotspots

Tourism and Hospitality Minister Louise Upston has committed $3.8 million from the International Visitor Conservation and Tourism Levy to upgrade public toilets in two of the country's most heavily visited districts, Mackenzie and Queenstown Lakes. The framing is squarely about who pays: "Local communities shouldn't be left to carry the full cost of facilities used by visitors from across New Zealand and around the world."

It is a small number against the scale of the pressure, but the principle is the point. Districts like Mackenzie, home to Aoraki and the dark-sky reserve, and Queenstown Lakes carry visitor volumes far larger than their ratepayer base can reasonably fund, and the levy exists precisely to close that gap. Watching where the IVL actually lands, toilets and car parks rather than marketing, is a fair read on how the current government sees the fund's job.

**Source:** [Beehive](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/relieving-pressure-facilities-tourism-hotspots)

## Wellington leans on its August festival as spending stays tight

The capital's hospitality operators are heading into their traditionally quietest month with the one thing that reliably fills tables: Visa Wellington On a Plate, which opens on 1 August and runs across the month. Festival director Beth Brash says the sector is proving resilient and quick to pivot, and points to the programme's biggest-ever regional spread this year, with strong growth in Kāpiti and the Hutt Valley.

The read underneath the optimism is a market watching its wallet. Brash notes that small indulgences, an iced matcha and a pastry rather than a full dinner out, are where discretionary spend is going, and the festival's own numbers lean on that: last year's events were credited with about $10 million and more than half of this year's tickets have already sold. For a city whose winter hospitality trade has softened with the wider economy, August is doing a lot of the annual heavy lifting.
