Napier Port Holdings is the NZX-listed company that runs the port at Ahuriri, Napier, the export gateway for Hawke's Bay and much of the central and lower North Island, and New Zealand's fourth largest port by container volume. It is worth a profile because it is the clearest public case in the visitor economy's neighbourhood of a business that stopped measuring itself by throughput and started measuring what each unit earns, and because its cruise line shows what the cruise slowdown looks like from the wharf.

## From harbour board to the sharemarket

The port describes itself as having worked for Hawke's Bay for more than 150 years. Until 2019 it was wholly owned by Hawke's Bay Regional Council. Needing a new wharf it could not fund from its own balance sheet, the council consulted and voted to sell 45 percent through a public float, keeping 55 percent through its investment company. The company listed on 20 August 2019, and the float raised $234 million: $110 million stayed with the port to repay debt and fund the wharf, and $108 million went to a council future fund.

The float was built to keep the port local. A priority offer went to residents, iwi entities and staff, and the company's 2019 annual report says nearly 8,000 locals bought in, two-thirds of them first-time share investors, and 97 percent of employees took shares, many with an interest-free staff share loan. The wharf the money paid for, Te Whiti, opened on 22 July 2022, below its budgeted construction range of $173 million to $190 million, according to the 2022 annual report.

## How it is built now

At 30 September 2025 Napier Port had 315 permanent employees (its diversity table counts 307 plus an eight-person senior team), six wharves with eight berths, three tugs and 74.7 hectares of port and off-site land. It works around the clock 364 days a year. Exports are 80 percent of cargo by weight, and the company says most of its exporters are within 100 kilometres of the wharf. Revenue for the year was $157.7 million, up 11.6 percent, audited by Ernst & Young on behalf of the Auditor-General:

The company's preferred earnings line, the result from operating activities (revenue less operating costs, before depreciation, interest and tax), rose 23.5 percent to $64.2 million. Reported profit after tax was $30.9 million, which includes the $7.5 million final settlement of its Cyclone Gabrielle insurance claim; without that and a $3.96 million asset write-down, the company's underlying figure was $28.3 million, up from $20.7 million. Dividends for the year came to 14.5 cents a share, up from 9 cents, and the council's investment company received $13.75 million of the $25 million paid out in the year.

## What is worth copying: price the unit, not the pile

The five-year summary in the annual report tells the story better than any single year. In 2021 the port moved 5.87 million tonnes of cargo, 276,129 containers (counted in twenty-foot equivalent units, TEU) and 3.95 million tonnes of bulk, for $109.5 million of revenue. In 2025 it moved 5.06 million tonnes, 250,454 TEU and 3.41 million tonnes of bulk, for $157.7 million. Total cargo fell 13.8 percent; revenue rose 44.1 percent.

Bulk did the same on a smaller scale: about $10.50 of revenue a tonne in 2021 and $15.08 in 2025, an increase of roughly 44 percent. The company attributes the gains to tariff increases, cargo mix, and selling more around each box, such as container depot work and its Port Pack service, which packs pulp and timber into containers on site and regularly fills more than 100 a day. The trend has not stopped: in the nine months to 30 June 2026 container revenue per TEU rose 17.7 percent to $439 on volume up just 1 percent.

Two smaller decisions are worth copying too. First, when cost rises slower than price, it shows: in 2025 revenue grew $16.4 million and operating costs grew $4.2 million. The company shared some of that with staff, paying $4,314 per eligible full-time employee in cash and shares, which keeps the 2019 idea of a port its own people own a stake in alive. Second, it bought its biggest new asset with a rival rather than against one. A $36 million dredge, due in service in late 2026, is owned through a partnership formed with Port Otago, in which Napier Port holds 30 percent. Two ports that each need a dredge a few months a year now share one.

## The cruise line, and why the port can shrug it off

Cruise is where Napier Port touches the visitor economy directly, and it is going the wrong way. Calls fell from 89 in 2024 to 78 in 2025, bringing about 107,000 passengers, and to 55 in the season that finished in April 2026, bringing more than 88,000. Cruise revenue fell from $9.07 million to $8.25 million to $6.5 million. The company had 50 bookings for the 2027 season when it reported in August.

The port has been doing to cruise what it does to cargo. In the 2020 year, 76 calls brought in $4.3 million, about $56,600 a call. In 2025 it was about $105,800 a call, and in 2026 about $118,200. Per passenger the figure is steadier, about $77 in 2025 and $74 in 2026. But price cannot fix the number of ships, which is set by cruise lines deciding how often New Zealand is worth the trip. At 5.2 percent of revenue the port can absorb the loss. The businesses ashore that sell to those passengers have no container trade to fall back on, and Napier Port's own numbers are the earliest public measure of how many fewer people will walk off the wharf next summer.

## Where the model runs out of road

**Yield has a ceiling.** Lifting price 60 percent per container in four years works because cargo owners near Napier have nowhere else close to send it. That is market power, and it has limits: a regional economy can only carry so many tariff rises before exporters reroute by road or rail to another port, or lose margin abroad. The 2021 starting point was also the busiest container year in the five-year table, so part of the volume fall is an unusual base rather than lost trade.

**A few customers and one industry carry a lot of it.** Two customers made up 14 percent and 12 percent of 2025 revenue, about $41 million between them. Logs were 66 percent of export weight. The company's own risk statement says it has "no ability to control" wood product supply or demand, and log exports fell 5.2 percent in the nine months to June 2026 as exporters faced higher costs.

**The weather is uninsurable at the scale that matters.** The port's Cyclone Gabrielle insurance claims, from February 2023, were not finally settled until the 2025 year. Its own risk statement says insurance does not provide complete protection and there is limited ability to engineer existing assets against major natural events.

**The next stage is borrowed.** A $120 million investment programme over 2025 to 2027 had deployed $70 million by June 2026. Drawn debt rose from $107 million at September 2025 to $136.5 million at June 2026, and undrawn facilities fell from $73 million to $43.5 million. Debt at 1.88 times operating earnings is still conservative, but the strong cash years are now going into concrete and a dredge rather than dividends, and the payoff is promised from the 2027 year.

**It cannot be bought, and it cannot easily sell itself.** With the regional council holding 55 percent, there is no takeover discipline, and any new capital raise has to suit a council's balance sheet as well as the market's.

The audited result for the year to 30 September 2026 has not been published yet. The company's August guidance was for an underlying operating result around the top of its $70 million to $74 million range.

## What to take away

If you run a tourism business that measures itself in heads, Napier Port is a case for counting dollars per head instead. It moves less than it did four years ago and earns far more, because it priced each unit deliberately, sold services around the core product, and kept cost growth under revenue growth. It also funded a step change by giving its own community and staff a stake, and shared the next big asset with a neighbour rather than duplicating it.

What a reader should be wary of is how much of that depends on being the only port nearby. Most visitor businesses have competitors a short drive away, and price rises that work at a regional monopoly will not work at a tour desk. The cruise line is the part of Napier Port that looks most like the rest of the industry: pricing up per ship, and still losing ships.
