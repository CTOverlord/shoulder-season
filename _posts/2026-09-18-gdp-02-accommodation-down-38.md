It's Friday. Yesterday's GDP release was a good headline built on a construction site, and underneath it the visitor economy had its worst quarter in a while. Here's what's moving.

## The economy grew 0.2 percent, and the industries that carry visitors were the ones that shrank

Stats NZ published GDP for the June 2026 quarter yesterday morning. Economic activity rose 0.2 percent, following a 0.9 percent rise in the March quarter. Bank economists had picked somewhere between 0.1 and 0.3 percent, and the Reserve Bank had forecast flat, so the number came in at the better end of expectations. On an annual average basis GDP rose 1.7 percent over the year ended June 2026, and the size of the economy was $455 billion.

Nine of the sixteen industries grew. Construction did the heavy lifting, up 2.7 percent, its largest quarterly increase since the June 2023 quarter, driven by residential building. Public administration and safety was next, up 2.0 percent.

Four industries made negative contributions to the quarter, and three of them sell to visitors. Here is the full contribution table, in percentage points of GDP:

Arts and recreation services, retail trade and accommodation, and transport, postal and warehousing are the three lines a tour operator, an attraction and a hotel sit in. They are the bottom three.

At industry level, retail trade and accommodation fell 1.0 percent, and Stats NZ names accommodation and food and beverage services as the drivers. The sub-industry number is worse than the group number lets on: accommodation and food services fell 3.8 percent in the quarter, which Stats NZ says indicates "a potential change in discretionary spending". Household spending on restaurants and hotels fell as well.

Transport, postal and warehousing fell 1.7 percent, driven by road transport and transport support services. Household spending on transport fell 1.6 percent.

Stats NZ is unusually direct about the cause. It says the quarter's GDP measures were affected by the Middle East conflict and the fuel price rises that followed. Quarterly price increases for petrol imports were the highest since December 1990, and for processed fuels and lubricants the highest on record in a series going back to September 1990. Fuel retailing fell in volume terms by the largest quarterly amount since the COVID-affected September 2021 quarter, which is what it looks like when people keep the car and drive it less.

Two more lines are worth holding onto. GDP per capita rose 0.1 percent, so the growth barely outran the population. And real gross national disposable income, which is closer to what the country can actually spend, fell 0.4 percent, because import prices rose 13.8 percent against export prices up 3.5 percent.

The one number that went the visitor economy's way is an import. Services imports fell 3.7 percent, with travel services down 6.4 percent. New Zealanders spent less on going overseas. That is money staying in the country, though on this evidence it is not being spent on a weekend away here either.

Reaction split along the lines you would expect. BNZ senior economist Doug Steel called it a stronger than expected economic pulse. Infometrics principal economist Brad Olsen said people still got out and did quite a bit. Finance Minister Nicola Willis pointed at construction and exporters. Labour's Barbara Edmonds pointed at real GDP per capita being lower than when her party left office. ACT leader David Seymour said the result was good but not fast enough.

The part that matters for anyone carrying debt against a vehicle fleet or a building: the Reserve Bank lifted the Official Cash Rate by 25 basis points to 2.75 percent this month, and a result that beat its own forecast is not an argument for stopping. The next review is on 28 October, with a full Monetary Policy Statement on 9 December.

## The Government puts $880,000 into a WOW exhibition that premieres in Wellington in 2027

Tourism and Hospitality Minister Louise Upston announced yesterday that $880,000 from the Major Events Fund will go to a new World of WearableArt exhibition, premiering in Wellington in 2027. It puts WOW's award-winning garments into a high-tech immersive format covering four decades of the competition, and it will tour internationally after the Wellington season, with those details due next year. A separate $400,000 from the Events Boost Fund supports this year's WOW event.

The release says the exhibition is expected to attract more than 1,500 international visitors, and that it has been timed to sit alongside the annual WOW Show, the first Global Wearable Art Summit and the Australian school holidays. An education programme comes with it.

Do the division and $880,000 across more than 1,500 international visitors is about $587 of public money each. That is the ceiling implied by the only visitor number in the release rather than the whole return, because the exhibition will also sell to New Zealanders, the summit brings delegates, and none of that is quantified. Which is the recurring problem with Major Events Fund announcements: the cost is exact and the benefit is a category. The Spinoff went through the fund's claims in March and found the same gap. WOW is a stronger candidate than most, in that it already pulls people to Wellington in a shoulder month and the garments already exist, so a permanent home for them is a reasonable thing to fund. It would simply be easier to judge with a published business case.

## Nineteen visitor visa categories change systems on Thursday, and old drafts are deleted on 8 October

From 24 September, new applications for nineteen visitor visa categories can only be started in Immigration New Zealand's enhanced Immigration Online system. The list covers the general Visitor Visa, Business Visitor, Parent and Grandparent, Medical Treatment, Sports Events, Visiting Media, Super Yachts, Private Yacht or Plane, and Crew Joining a Ship or Aircraft, among others. Two stay where they are: Partner of a New Zealander and Child of a New Zealander.

Inbound operators have already been through their part of this. The three categories the trade actually uses, the ADS Group Visitor Visa, the Group Visitor Visa and the Tour Escort Visitor Visa, moved across on 24 August.

The date to diary is the second one. Drafts already started in the old system can still be finished and submitted there, but on 8 October the old forms close and any remaining drafts are deleted. Anyone holding a half-finished application after that starts again in the new system. INZ says it is contacting draft holders directly, which is worth checking rather than relying on, particularly for anyone who set up an application for a guest months out from travel.

## Ngāti Kuri are running their own tours to Te Rerenga Wairua, four a week

Ngāti Kuri have started operating visitor experiences through their rohe at the top of the North Island, running from Kā Uri out to Te Rerenga Wairua. The tours are guided by local whānau and built around karakia, waiata and the stories attached to the sites, rather than a commentary on the coastline going past. Steffan Panoho, programme coordinator at Kā Uri, describes the purpose as explaining the wairuatanga of the place to visitors. Tourism operations manager Joe Waru says people are increasingly after an indigenous perspective on the places they travel to.

Four tours run each week at present, on boats seating 18, and the aim is to build to daily. That arithmetic is the interesting part. Eighteen seats across four departures is at most 72 people a week, which is a rounding error against the coaches that have been driving to Cape Reinga for decades. It is also the whole point. The constraint is deliberate, the operator owns the story it is selling, and the value stays in the rohe rather than passing through it. The question the model has to answer is whether daily departures can be staffed without thinning out what makes the four worth taking.

## The chair of New Zealand Māori Tourism is standing for National in Christchurch Central

Dale Aotea Stephens, chair of New Zealand Māori Tourism, is contesting Christchurch Central for National at the 7 November election, and sits 23rd on the party's list. A list place in the low twenties for a party currently in government is the kind of number that usually converts, so the sector should probably plan on losing a chair and gaining a member of Parliament rather than one or the other.

What that means for Māori tourism depends on a question the reporting does not answer, which is what happens to the chair's seat and when. A national body's chair campaigning for a party during an election is a governance knot whichever party it is, because the body has to keep working with whoever wins. There is nothing improper on the face of it. It is simply a job that is hard to do twice at once.
