It's Tuesday, and a milestone six years in the waiting turns out to be worth about a thousand people. Here's what's moving.

## July's overseas arrivals beat the same month of 2019 for the first time

Stats NZ counted 256,638 overseas visitor arrivals in July 2026, rounded in its release to 256,600. That is up 20,100, or 8.5 percent, on July 2025, and Stats NZ says it is the first month in which arrivals have exceeded their pre-COVID level against the same month of 2019.

The milestone is real, and it is narrow:

- July 2026: 256,638
- July 2019: 255,585
- Difference: 1,053 people, or 0.4 percent

Two markets did most of the lifting. Australia sent 134,900, a record for any July and 8,200 more than the previous record of 126,700 set last July, which made it 53 percent of the month's arrivals. China was up 5,400, or 25 percent. Together that is 8,200 plus 5,400, or 13,600 of the 20,100 increase: about two thirds of the growth from two countries. July is ski season, so the Australian share is the winter product selling to its nearest market, which is exactly what it is supposed to do.

The annual number has not crossed yet. There were 3.69 million arrivals in the year to July, up 305,000 or 9 percent, against 3.89 million in the year to July 2019. That leaves about 200,000 to go, or roughly 95 percent recovered. On the June-year figures published a month ago the gap was 215,400, so it is closing, at something like 15,000 a month.

The other half of the release is worth a glance. New Zealand residents came home from 316,974 overseas trips in July, down from 321,946 a year earlier and 340,250 in July 2019. That is 316,974 divided by 340,250, or 93 percent of the 2019 level. In the month overseas visitors finally passed 2019, New Zealanders were still travelling abroad less than they did then, which is a quiet help to anyone selling domestic winter trips.

Tourism and Hospitality Minister Louise Upston described the figures as progress towards doubling the value of tourism exports by 2034. This release counts people, not spending, so it cannot test that claim either way.

## Auckland's first rail weekday added 25,000 trips, and most of the growth came after the morning rush

Auckland Transport estimated about 72,500 passengers on trains across its network by 5pm yesterday, the City Rail Link's first full weekday, which is 25,000 more than at the same time the previous Monday. By 9am the count was about 24,000, against about 19,000 a week earlier. Auckland Transport put morning peak punctuality at 99 percent of trains within five minutes of schedule, although RNZ reports a train fault caused several cancellations during the morning.

Split the day at 9am and the interesting part shows up:

- Previous Monday, to 5pm: 72,500 minus 25,000, or about 47,500
- Before 9am: 24,000 against 19,000, up about 5,000 or 26 percent
- 9am to 5pm this Monday: 72,500 minus 24,000, or 48,500
- 9am to 5pm the previous Monday: 47,500 minus 19,000, or 28,500
- Daytime growth: 48,500 against 28,500, up 20,000 or 70 percent

The commute grew by a quarter. The daytime, which belongs to shoppers, day-trippers and visitors, grew by more than two thirds. Chief executive Stacey van der Putten read it as people "exploring the new routes that CRL offers". Some of that will be novelty, and one Monday is not a trend. But these are the hours in which a hotel near Te Waihorotiu or Karanga-a-Hape can start telling guests to take the train, and on the first weekday people were already doing it.

## Marriott's first flagship-brand hotel in New Zealand will be in Rotorua, and all three of its new signings are conversions

Marriott International announced agreements with CP Group, the owner of the Fable hotels, for three North Island properties last Thursday:

- Rotorua Marriott Hotel: 135 rooms, converted from the Arawa Park Hotel on Fenton Street, reopening under the Marriott Hotels brand in late 2027 after a 12-month refurbishment
- The Official Auckland, Autograph Collection: 82 rooms in a historic building on Eden Crescent, due in 2028
- Fable Wellington, Autograph Collection: 118 rooms in a 1940s office building on the corner of Lambton Quay and Stout Street, due in 2029
- Total: 135 plus 82 plus 118, or 335 rooms

The first thing to notice is that none of these is a new build. Two are existing buildings and one is an existing hotel, so this is mostly rooms changing their flag and joining a global loyalty programme, not a lift in national room supply. For the rest of a destination that still matters: a Marriott or Autograph name puts a property in front of travellers who book almost entirely inside one chain's system, and some of those travellers will be buying activities in the same town.

The second is where the flagship brand lands. Marriott currently operates two hotels in New Zealand, both in Auckland, according to The Hotel Conversation. Its first Marriott Hotels-branded property will be in Rotorua, not Queenstown. Marriott's Richard Crawford called the inaugural hotels in Wellington and Rotorua "outstanding outcomes". The release describes Rotorua as reopening, which suggests 135 rooms come out of the town's supply for around a year, but no closure dates have been given.

## A Northland Great Ride gets $1 million of visitor levy money, and ratepayers are putting in twice that

Tourism and Hospitality Minister Louise Upston announced $1 million from the International Visitor Conservation and Tourism Levy yesterday for Pou Herenga Tai Twin Coast Cycle Trail, the 87-kilometre, two-day Great Ride between Kaikohe and Ōpua. It pays for the first stage of a new section between Ōpua and the Whangae Tunnel, plus a longer-term project to improve the link between Ōpua and Taumarere, where the Bay of Islands Vintage Railway Trust has reinstated the railway. Far North District Council is putting in a further $2 million on top of its earlier investment in the trail.

The split is what sets this apart from Saturday's Kaikōura grant, where the release gave no council share at all:

- Levy money: $1 million
- Far North District Council: $2 million
- Total new funding: $3 million, of which the levy is one third
- Against the trail's estimated contribution to Northland's economy of $22.8 million a year, $3 million is about 13 percent of one year's return

That is a modest ask for an asset of that size, and it lands in the middle of the election argument over whether levy money should flow through councils. Here a council is already spending twice the levy's contribution on the same project. Construction is expected to start this financial year, which for a council ends on 30 June 2027, once the final design is settled.
