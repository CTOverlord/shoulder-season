It's Thursday, and the government has quietly acknowledged that a lot of trip planning no longer happens on anybody's website. Here's what's moving.

## The government is paying to make New Zealand legible to AI search

Tourism and Hospitality Minister Louise Upston announced yesterday that $800,000 from the International Visitor Conservation and Tourism Levy will go into rebuilding how Tourism New Zealand structures its data. Two pieces: making the database holding information on thousands of tourism and hospitality businesses AI-ready so that machines can read it cleanly, and expanding Tourism New Zealand's existing AI travel assistant with more detailed regional tourism organisation content. The stated aim is that New Zealand shows up well when someone asks a chatbot where to go in November.

It is a small number attached to a real shift. The discovery layer is moving from a search results page, where a destination could buy or earn its way to the top, to a generated answer, where an assistant summarises a handful of sources and the user never sees the rest. If your product data is unstructured, contradictory across listings, or simply absent from the places these systems read, you do not rank badly. You are not in the answer at all. That is a different failure mode and most operators have no way of knowing it is happening to them.

The practical read for anyone running a business: the government is fixing the national layer, not yours. What is under your control is whether your own information is consistent and machine-readable across your site, your regional tourism organisation listing, your booking platform and Google's business profile, and whether the operating details an assistant needs to recommend you, duration, price, departure point, season, accessibility, are actually stated in text rather than sitting in a PDF or a hero image. The $800,000 raises the odds New Zealand gets mentioned. Whether you get mentioned is a housekeeping job nobody is funding.

## The Woodend bypass clears consent, and North Canterbury gets four years of roadworks

The SH1 Belfast to Pegasus Motorway and Woodend Bypass has been granted fast-track approval, the 27th project through the process and only the second road. NZTA lodged in December 2025 and the panel took about four months. The project runs roughly 11 kilometres from south of the Kaiapoi River bridge to just north of the Pegasus and Ravenwood intersection, with an investment envelope of $800 million to $1 billion, and NZTA's board has already released $68.1 million for detailed design and early works plus about $37 million for property. Main construction is expected to start later this year and run four years. The safety case is the strongest part of it: predicted deaths and serious injuries on the stretch fall from 5.6 a year to 1.25.

For the visitor economy this is the road, not a road. Everything heading north out of Christchurch uses it: Kaikōura, Hanmer, the Waipara and Weka Pass wineries, the Picton ferry, and every whale watch transfer and Alpine Pacific self-drive itinerary sold offshore. Woodend is currently the pinch point where 21,500 vehicles a day meet a village main street, and it is projected to reach 28,000 by 2048.

The catch is the sequencing. Four years of construction on a single-corridor state highway means the pain arrives before the benefit, and it starts in the same window as the Christchurch international capacity ramp-up that is meant to put more rental cars on that road. Anyone quoting 2027 and 2028 transfer times north of the city should be building in a buffer now rather than explaining one later.

## Otago gets $3.9 million for predators, which is the other half of yesterday's problem

Conservation Minister Tama Potaka announced $3.9 million over three years for predator control from Port Chalmers to the Orokonui Ecosanctuary, across the Aramoana peninsula, Blueskin Bay and the Otago Peninsula, targeting stoats, feral cats and possums. It funds removal work, tighter biosecurity on the peninsula, and a trial of AI-enabled traps with faster detection and response. It runs through Predator Free Dunedin alongside DOC, iwi, landowners, researchers and volunteers, and follows $5.5 million for Wellington and $10 million for Auckland under Predator Free 2050.

Read it against Tuesday's set-net decision and the shape of the problem becomes clearer. The researchers who put mainland hoiho at fewer than 115 breeding pairs were explicit that bycatch is the smallest of the three pressures, behind disease, predation and starvation. Fisheries rules addressed one. This addresses the second. Nobody has a lever for the gyrovirus yet.

Commercially, the peninsula is the most productive wildlife-viewing asset in the lower South Island and one of the few genuinely twelve-month propositions Dunedin sells. Three years of funding on stoats and cats is not a fix, but it is the difference between managing a decline and watching one.

## Wānaka moves to shut two freedom camping spots, with submissions open to 31 August

Queenstown Lakes District Council opened consultation on Monday on three changes to its Freedom Camping Bylaw 2025, all in Wānaka. Freedom camping would be permanently prohibited at the Allenby Place carpark, banned overnight at the Beacon Point Road carpark through the December to February peak, and the Wānaka Recreation Centre would go from 18 designated spaces to 21. The council's reasoning is that the impacts experienced at Allenby Place and Beacon Point suggest additional controls are needed at those two sites. Submissions close at 5pm on Monday 31 August, then a hearing panel reports back to council.

Note the arithmetic: two sites closed or restricted, three extra spaces added. That is a net reduction in legal capacity in a district where campervan traffic is rebounding, and the vehicles do not stop arriving because a carpark changes status. They move to the next one, which is how a site-by-site bylaw turns into a rolling series of consultations. The honest version of this policy is that a lakefront town wanting freedom campers managed rather than merely displaced has to fund somewhere for them to go, and three spaces at the rec centre is not that. Worth a submission from anyone in the rental or holiday park trade before the end of August.

## The empty Smith and Caughey's building reopens as a ticketed attraction

Titanic: The Human Story, the Musealia exhibition presented by Fever, makes its New Zealand debut in the Smith and Caughey's building on Queen Street from 28 August, with tickets on sale from 5 August and adult entry starting at $40. It carries more than 200 original artefacts and personal effects from passengers and crew alongside built recreations of parts of the ship, and has drawn more than three million visitors across runs including London, New York, Washington, Brisbane and Sydney. Auckland Live, Auckland Museum, the New Zealand Maritime Museum and Auckland Council Events are all named as supporting it.

The venue is the story. Smith and Caughey's traded on Queen Street for over a century and closed for the last time on 15 June 2025, leaving a Category 1 heritage building empty on the city's main retail spine at exactly the point Auckland was arguing about whether that spine still had a purpose. Filling it with a paid international exhibition for a season is a more useful answer than another consultation, and it gives the CBD a wet-weather, mid-week, non-summer reason to exist that does not depend on a cruise ship being in.

It is also a template worth noticing. Touring exhibitions are effectively rented demand: someone else has built the product, taken the risk and proved the audience, and a city with vacant floorspace supplies the room. For regional centres with an empty anchor tenant and a shoulder season to fill, that trade is more available than it looks.
