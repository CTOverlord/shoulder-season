It's Thursday, and the South Island's summer just got a schedule worth planning around. Here's what's moving.

## Christchurch Airport has 1.27 million international seats booked for summer, and long-haul is up 54 percent

Christchurch Airport published its summer schedule on Wednesday: 1.27 million international seats between November and March, 21 percent more than last summer. The split matters more than the total. Long-haul is up 54 percent, about 130,000 extra seats. Tasman and Pacific Island capacity is up 11 percent, about 89,000.

Air New Zealand's three new 787-9 routes account for roughly 92,250 of those seats on their own: Singapore from 28 October (34,300), Tokyo Narita from 28 November (29,100) and Perth from 30 November (28,850). The incumbents grew harder than the newcomers in places. China Southern is up 57 percent to 103,350 seats on Guangzhou, running up to 10 flights a week from December to February. Singapore Airlines is up 21 percent to 120,400, Cathay Pacific up 15 percent to 58,050 on Hong Kong, Qantas up 9 percent to 136,400 on Sydney with its third daily service stretched from six weeks to 14. Jetstar adds Perth as a new route (29,700) and more Sydney flying (32,000). On 22 November there will be 1,560 seats moving between Christchurch and Singapore in a single day.

The extended seasons are the part worth reading twice. A South Island inbound season that used to compress into December, January and February is being pulled out at both ends by airlines choosing to keep aircraft on the route rather than redeploy them. Chief executive Justin Watson framed it as a capital-allocation vote: airlines have choices about where the aircraft go, and frequency, aircraft size and season length are how they express confidence.

## Methven has fewer than 1000 homes and up to 170 short-term lets

RNZ spent time in Methven this week and came back with the arithmetic that makes the argument. Average property values climbed from $380,000 to $640,000 between 2018 and 2024, close to 70 percent. The last census found more than 13 percent of the town's homes unoccupied. Ashburton District Council estimates 130 to 170 Airbnbs and other short-term lets in a town of fewer than 1000 houses, and a search of the platform turns up more than 200 listings.

The council can only estimate because of how the rules are drawn. Group manager compliance and development Ian Hyde says short-term letting is a permitted activity in residential zones up to five guests a night, and 10 in rural zones, with no registration and no neighbour approval required. Anything larger is generally discretionary and needs land use consent. So the small operators are invisible to the council by design, and the large ones only appear when someone applies.

That threshold is the whole fight. Homeowner Paul Dixey's objection is not to weekend letting but to houses built or bought to sleep 12 or 15 sitting inside residential streets, and to owners applying for consent after the fact. Methven Motels and Apartments owner Warren Nisbet makes the commercial-accommodation case: building warrant of fitness, an annual fire service visit, monthly alarm checks and a yearly fire evacuation plan are costs a house next door does not carry. Mt Hutt ski area manager James McKenzie argues the other way, that the bed base short-term letting created is what makes Methven choosable at all in a town with no year-round trade to sustain a hotel. Community board chair Megan Fitzgerald says the council is working through policy and nobody wants a ghost town.

Every district plan in the country sets that permitted-activity number, and most set it without thinking of it as visitor accommodation policy. Methven is the version of this argument that arrives early.

## The DOC Community Fund had $9.2 million to give and $69 million of requests

Conservation Minister Tama Potaka announced the 2026 round on Wednesday: $9.2 million to 20 community groups. The round drew 121 applications seeking more than $69 million, so roughly seven dollars of work was proposed for every dollar available. The successful groups are expected to add about $12 million of their own in co-funding, donations and volunteer time, which means the Crown contribution is closer to a third of the project value than all of it.

The list runs from internationally recognised wetlands and geothermal areas to Northland horopito and Whitaker's skink. In Canterbury, Te Taumutu Rūnanga takes $521,733 to restore the wetland at Muriwai o Whata, better known as Coopers Lagoon. The two Chatham Islands projects worth $1.7 million, covered here yesterday, came out of the same round.

For anyone selling nature to visitors, the oversubscription ratio is the number to hold on to. A large share of the predator control, wetland restoration and track-adjacent habitat work that makes the estate worth visiting is done by volunteer groups on contestable funding, and this year eight applications in 10 went away with nothing.

## Four-wheel drives tore up Klondyke Corner, and DOC is asking the public for names

Aerial photographs show extensive damage across Klondyke Corner campsite in Arthur's Pass National Park, believed to have been done by four-wheel drive vehicles on 1 August, just before 4pm. DOC operations manager Leeann Ellis says the vehicles left deep tyre tracks over a large area, compacted the soil, cut wide ruts that will erode in heavy rain, destroyed plants and opened the ground to weeds. DOC is working with police and wants information from the public.

Driving off formed roads in a national park is illegal, as is damaging its natural or historic features, and offenders face infringement fines or prosecution. Ellis was careful to separate the activity from the offence, pointing four-wheel drivers at the Edwards Valley and Maling Pass tracks in the St James Conservation Area instead.

Klondyke Corner is free, unbooked and unstaffed, which is exactly why it is exposed. It also draws thousands of people a year and fills with Coast to Coast competitors and support crews every February, so the repair bill lands on a site an event depends on.

## Auckland Airport reports its full year this morning, and depreciation is the story

Auckland Airport releases its result for the 12 months to 30 June 2026 today, with chief executive Carrie Hurihanganui and chief financial officer Stewart Reynolds on a webcast at 11am. At the time of writing the numbers were not yet out.

The frame is already set. Guidance opened at underlying profit after tax of $280 million to $320 million and was narrowed in February to $295 million to $320 million, on about 8.6 million domestic and 10.6 million international passenger movements and capital expenditure of $1.0 billion to $1.2 billion. The first half delivered revenue of $519.6 million, up 4 percent, operating EBITDAFI of $371.3 million, up 6 percent, and underlying profit after tax of $157.1 million, up 6 percent, on 9.64 million passengers.

Set that against FY25, where underlying profit after tax rose 12 percent to $310.4 million on revenue of $1,004.7 million. The midpoint of FY26 guidance is roughly flat on that, with more passengers moving through the airport. The company said why a year ago: the depreciation attached to a billion dollars a year of building has started arriving in the profit line. Two things worth watching in the detail are whether domestic passenger numbers stopped going backwards, and whether the capital expenditure guidance for FY27 moves.
