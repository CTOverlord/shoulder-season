## The Commerce Commission files charges against Air New Zealand over delay compensation

The Commerce Commission announced on Thursday that it will file charges in the Auckland District Court under the Fair Trading Act, alleging Air New Zealand misled passengers about their right to compensation when flights were disrupted for reasons within the airline's control. That means engineering faults, crew sickness and operational delays, not weather.

The Commission alleges the airline told customers compensation was strictly capped at amounts lower than the legal limits, and made statements limiting its liability for some kinds of reimbursement. It believes the statements were made from January 2024. Under the Civil Aviation Act, a domestic passenger delayed for a reason within the airline's control can claim reasonable costs such as meals, accommodation and the cost of getting to their destination, up to ten times the ticket price. Deputy chair Anne Callinan said consumers had "frequently raised concerns" about the airline over five years.

Air New Zealand said it was disappointed by the decision to go to court and had cooperated in good faith. It told RNZ it had voluntarily reviewed more than 62,000 claims for disruptions within its control, found mistakes in fewer than one percent, contacted those customers and fixed them. Consumer NZ chief executive Jon Duffy pointed to Jetstar's recent $2.25 million penalty in a similar case and argued New Zealand is an outlier in not requiring airlines to tell passengers their rights when a disruption happens. For operators, the practical point is that a guest stranded overnight by a mechanical delay on a domestic sector has a statutory claim against the airline, and the airline's own wording is now being tested in court.

## Rotorua Museum has its building back and $5.6 million of its exhibitions still to fund

The $73.35 million construction phase of Rotorua Museum's restoration finished in September, a decade after the Bath House closed over earthquake safety concerns that followed the Kaikōura quake. Filling it is another matter. Of the $9.51 million budgeted for exhibitions, $5.6 million had still not been found at the council's late-October update, almost 60 percent of the total, according to Local Democracy Reporting.

Mayor Tania Tapsell says the gap will be closed without new money from ratepayers, whose contribution was capped at $15.5 million. The council is selling an inner-city asset it will not name, with the sale expected by the end of the year, and is asking the Bay of Plenty Regional Council's new infrastructure fund for $2 million. Council staff were more careful: none of these sources can be treated as secured, funding is a "key programme risk", and the target reopening stays at August 2028.

For Rotorua's visitor economy the building matters more than its balance sheet. Tapsell noted that museums do not make money but that domestic and international visitors are waiting for it, and Government Gardens is already busy after its own makeover. The museum opens for a free community day on Saturday, with building tours and a preview of the exhibitions, and builder Watts & Hughes will match donations up to $10,000.

## Another Courtenay Place venue closes, and the developers are planning for daytime

Dakota, a Courtenay Place bar that ran for 14 years, closed earlier this month. Owner Jose Ubiaga told RNZ the strip was "the worst it's ever been", blaming rent and alcohol licence costs; Wellington City Council's annual licence charges run from about $870 to $9,360 including the application fee. The bar had its licence suspended earlier this year for a second sale to a 16-year-old, which Ubiaga said had nothing to do with the closure. RNZ lists The Garden Hotel, Red Square, BL3 and Siglo among other recent closures in the area.

Hospitality NZ chief executive Kristy Phillips backed the Ministry for Regulation's September recommendation to replace council-by-council licensing fees with one national system. Meanwhile development group Te Aro Hā, working with the council's heritage regeneration fund, wants to turn the street into what its spokesperson called an "18-hour neighbourhood", with a boutique hostel, a sauna studio and a lighting store among the new tenants, and $150,000 from the council's City Growth Fund for lighting and art. The redeveloped Reading Cinemas building, due to open in late 2027, is the piece that would change the block. For the capital's evening economy, which visitors judge a city on, the gap until then is the problem.

## The first takahē chick hatches at Farewell Spit, and DOC asks visitors to stay on the tracks

Eight takahē were moved from Gouland Downs to the Onetahua Restoration Project at Farewell Spit in May, into a 2,000-hectare habitat behind a 4km predator-resistant fence. One pair that had not met before they arrived has produced a chick, hatched in October and now about six weeks old. Other attempts failed: one nest washed away and another egg was infertile.

DOC's Golden Bay operations manager Ross Trotter told RNZ that people have been going looking for the chick, and asked them not to. At eight to 12 weeks it will start moving into the open, where hawks and falcons become the risk, and the parents are keeping it hidden until then. Manawhenua Ki Mohua will help name it once it is clear it will survive. A fenced breeding population at the top of the South Island is a long-term asset for Golden Bay, and the request to give it room is the first test of how that asset and its visitors get along.

## Queenstown's Lakeview site held 12,669 tonnes of asbestos-contaminated soil

Documents released by Queenstown Lakes District Council on Thursday show far more asbestos was found at the Lakeview site than earlier investigations had identified. The final demolition report records 64.56 tonnes of asbestos-containing building material and 12,669 tonnes of contaminated soil removed over 12 months. Lakeview is the residential, hospitality and retail precinct being built on the edge of the town centre by Australian developer 94 Feet, a project of more than $1 billion.

The land sold to the developer has been remediated. The council says other land it has kept, including Lynch Block, Market Square and the proposed hot pool site, may need further investigation and possibly remediation. The council has a $75 million staged land deal with a share of the profit, so whatever that work costs comes off a return ratepayers are counting on, and the hot pool site is the part of the precinct with the clearest visitor role.
