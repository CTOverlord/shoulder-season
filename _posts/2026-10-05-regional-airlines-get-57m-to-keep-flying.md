It's Monday, the first full week of the election campaign, and the money on the table today is for the small planes. Five items. Here's what's moving.

## Regional airlines get $5.7 million in grants, not loans, to cover fuel and wages

Regional Development Minister Shane Jones and Associate Transport Minister James Meager announced this morning that $5.7 million from the Regional Infrastructure Fund will go out as working capital grants to regional airlines. Each grant will be sized on the airline's annual passenger numbers and flight movements, from $300,000 to $1.2 million, and is meant to cover day-to-day costs such as fuel and wages over the next 12 months. The condition is the same one attached to the earlier loans: airlines are expected to keep their current service levels. Jones put the pressure down to jet fuel and aviation gas prices rising more than 50 percent since March.

The shift matters more than the sum. Until now the regional air connectivity package, set up last year, has lent money: nearly $26 million across six airlines for aircraft, maintenance and refinancing, most of it to Air Chathams. Grants are an admission that some of these operators cannot carry more debt, and that the routes are being treated as a public service rather than a business that only needs a bridge. Jones called the support "temporary, targeted and proportionate". With grants running out in a year and fuel showing no sign of falling, the question for visitor-dependent places on these routes, from Stewart Island to Golden Bay, is what happens in October 2027.

## Auckland Airport is halfway through its $3.9 billion terminal rebuild, and the disruption now moves to where passengers can see it

Auckland Airport says more than half of its terminal integration programme has been delivered and the whole thing remains on track for 2029. In the 2026 financial year it commissioned nearly $1 billion of new aeronautical assets, including a 250,000-square-metre expansion of the northern airfield. The first change travellers will notice is Zone C in the centre of the international check-in hall, refurbished over six months with 28 self-service kiosks and 15 automatic bag drops in place of airline desks dating from the 1990s. It reopens later this year once airlines finish testing, and the next zone then goes behind hoardings. The equipment is shared rather than assigned to one airline, which is the start of a move to common-use check-in across the terminal.

Chief executive Carrie Hurihanganui was frank about the next phase: new facade, lifts, escalators, a new baggage system and changes to security and passport control, all built while the terminal stays open. She said it "will be frustrating at times". The airport says the integrated terminal will give about 26 percent more capacity than today's, and puts the programme's economic benefit at $4.5 billion a year, its own estimate. For anyone timing departures for groups or connecting domestic visitors to long-haul flights, the useful message is that the walk between terminals is going away, but not before three years of changing routes through the building.

## The Greens want an overnight sleeper from Auckland to Wellington and passenger trains south of Christchurch

The Green Party has made intercity passenger rail part of its transport platform. Three services are named: an overnight sleeper train between Auckland and Wellington, Te Huia extended from Hamilton to Tauranga through the Kaimai tunnel, and a passenger service on the Main South Line between Christchurch and Dunedin. Transport spokesperson Julie Anne Genter's argument is that "these tracks are already there", so the cost is stations, passing loops, signalling, tunnel ventilation and new sleeper carriages, plus operating money, with fares meant to cover the bulk of running costs. The party says Infometrics reviewed its costings and found them reasonable, but the figures themselves were not in the release.

Election promises on rail have a long history of staying on paper, and this one has no published price yet. The visitor angle is real all the same. An overnight sleeper is a visitor product in its own right, not just a way of moving commuters, and a Christchurch to Dunedin train would restore a link the South Island has not had since 2002. It also puts the opposition on the record about trains just as KiwiRail reports more people on its tourist services for less profit.

## A Northland dry dock for the new Cook Strait ferries clears its first fast-track hurdle

The Environmental Protection Authority has accepted the fast-track application for the Northland Shipyard and Dry Dock at Marsden Point as complete, Regional Development Minister Shane Jones said this morning. The application now goes to an independent expert panel. Cabinet has ring-fenced $100 million from the Regional Infrastructure Fund for the project, which is meant to let large vessels be maintained in New Zealand rather than in Sydney or Singapore.

The visitor economy's interest is in the ferries. Jones tied the dock directly to the new inter-island ferries, and the reliability of the Cook Strait crossing depends in part on how long ships are away for servicing. Interislander faces single-ship dry dock periods in the years ahead, and every week a ship spends sailing to and from Australia for maintenance is a week of reduced capacity. Acceptance as complete is a procedural step, not a consent, and the panel's decision is still to come.

## WiT is coming back to Queenstown in March 2028

Web in Travel, the Singapore-founded travel technology conference, will hold a second Queenstown edition on 28 March 2028, following a sold-out first event this year run with Technology Queenstown. The programme will cover artificial intelligence, digital experiences and distribution. Technology Queenstown, a privately funded not-for-profit set up in 2024, wants technology to grow from under 2 percent of the district's GDP to more than 15 percent over 20 years, and chair Roger Sharp pitched the town as a real-world test bed with "millions of visitors" coming through each year.

The two-year gap is worth noting: this is not yet an annual fixture. But a recurring travel-tech event in a resort town gives New Zealand operators a seat in the conversation about distribution and AI without a flight to Singapore, and it is the clearest sign yet that Queenstown means to be more than a place where tourism happens.
