It's Friday, and the day's biggest decision is one that stops something happening rather than starts it. Here's what's moving.

## DOC has closed new marine mammal permits at Banks Peninsula for ten more years, and widened the area it covers

The Department of Conservation announced today that it will not issue new marine mammal permits around Banks Peninsula / Te Pātāka-o-Rākaihautū for another ten years. The existing moratorium was due to expire next Wednesday, 16 September, so this is a rollover rather than a new restriction.

The part that is new is the map. The old moratorium covered Akaroa Harbour. The new one covers all waters around the peninsula, from the mouth of the Avon-Heathcote Estuary / Ihutai around to Birdlings Flat. DOC's reasoning is that the research on vessel interactions shows behavioural and habitat effects on Hector's dolphins, and that the feeding and socialising areas worth protecting are outside the harbour as well as in it.

Six operators hold permits now, working out of Akaroa and Lyttelton, and none of them are affected. That is the commercial story here. Those six permits have just become the only way to run a marine mammal tour on that coast until 2036, over a stretch of water several times the size of the one the moratorium used to fence off. A permit that was already scarce is now scarcer and covers more ground, which makes it an asset rather than a licence. Anyone who had been eyeing the Lyttelton side as cruise volumes shift around Canterbury has had that door shut this morning.

DOC's Mahaanui operations manager Andy Thompson framed it as keeping both things alive at once, saying the aim is to protect the species "while allowing sustainable tourism". Worth noting that DOC reports broad support from the operators themselves, which is what you would expect from six incumbents being handed a decade of certainty.

## The June year finished 215,000 arrivals short of the number the Government wanted in 2026

Stats NZ counted 3,674,600 overseas visitor arrivals in the June 2026 year, up 299,300 or 9 percent on the June 2025 year. Australia supplied 1.59 million of them, the United States 385,500, China 315,400 and the United Kingdom 196,500. Australia and China between them accounted for more than two thirds of the year's increase.

Set that against the Tourism Growth Roadmap, which has two numbers in it. The near one is a return to 2019's 3.89 million arrivals by 2026. The far one is doubling the 2023 value of tourism exports by 2034, from $9.9 billion to about $19.8 billion, with arrivals at roughly 4.78 million.

The near target is 215,400 arrivals away, which at the pace of the year just gone is about nine months of growth. It will be met late rather than missed.

The far target is the one worth doing the arithmetic on, because the two halves of it are not the same size of ask:

- Arrivals go from 3,674,600 to 4,780,000. That is 1.30 times, or 30 percent, spread over eight years.
- Export value goes from $9.9 billion to $19.8 billion. That is 2.00 times, or 100 percent, spread over eleven years from the 2023 base.
- Divide one by the other and spend per arrival has to rise by a factor of 1.54, or 54 percent, in nominal dollars.
- Over the eleven years from 2023, 54 percent compounds out at about 4.0 percent a year.

So the growth in arrivals the roadmap needs after 2027 is slower than what the country has just delivered, and the value doubling is, in nominal terms, close to a bet that spend per visitor keeps pace with inflation. The headline says double. The mechanism underneath is modest visitor growth plus prices holding their own. That is a more achievable target than the word doubling suggests, and a less impressive one.

## The Regional Infrastructure Fund put $8 million into keeping the Waitangi Treaty Grounds running

Regional Development Minister Shane Jones and Māori Development Minister Tama Potaka announced $8 million from the Regional Infrastructure Fund yesterday for the Waitangi Treaty Grounds. It goes to heritage building maintenance, museum operations, cultural facilities, public access infrastructure, and the educational and commemorative programmes on the site. The Treaty Grounds draw more than 160,000 visitors a year.

The interesting feature is what the money is for. Most of what an infrastructure fund does is build things. This is largely about keeping an existing attraction open and maintained, which is the cost line that visitor attractions with a national obligation and a regional catchment struggle hardest to carry.

It also brings the running total at and around the site to about $34.3 million of regional development money: $8 million now, $10.2 million from the same fund for infrastructure upgrades, $1.57 million for sealing and upgrading Haruru Falls Road, and $14.5 million from the old Provincial Growth Fund for Te Rau Aroha, the Māori Battalion museum. Jones tied the announcement to the 2040 bicentenary, which is the horizon everything at Waitangi is now being planned against.

## Conservation Week 2027 has been dated seven months out, and it lands in the autumn shoulder

DOC has set Conservation Week 2027 for Monday 19 April to Sunday 25 April, under the theme "Nature loves a team effort". It is the first for Pete Chrisp as director-general, and the pitch is group activity rather than individual: workplaces, community groups, whānau, tree planting, trapping, beach clean-ups.

Two things make this worth a diary line rather than a shrug. The week sits in the autumn shoulder, immediately after the summer season ends and while the weather in most of the country is still usable, which is the part of the calendar operators most need a reason to sell. And it finishes on ANZAC Day, so the back half runs into a long weekend.

Seven months of notice is the useful bit. A national nature campaign with dates this early is something a small operator can actually build a product around, rather than discovering it three weeks out and posting about it.
