## Netflix sets up a six-month shoot in Queenstown

Netflix has confirmed its first locally commissioned New Zealand series, an eight-episode drama named after the town it is set and shot in, Queenstown. The cast is led by Emmy nominee Rufus Sewell alongside Frances O'Connor, Alycia Debnam-Carey and New Zealand's Te Kohe Tuhaka, in a story built around a privileged family and the people who work for them in a luxury ski town.

The number that matters for the visitor economy is the footprint. Netflix puts the local economic impact at around $40 million and the production at roughly 450 cast and crew jobs over five months, with about 120 local crew engaged and filming running through to Christmas. The New Zealand Film Commission backed the early location scouting and made the introductions to Tourism New Zealand and the regional film and tourism offices, which is the quiet part of the deal: a globally distributed series set in a named New Zealand town is a destination-marketing asset that no campaign budget could buy outright. The screen-tourism payoff, if it lands, arrives on release rather than now.

## Fiordland absorbs a sizeable quake with the tourist roads open

A magnitude 6.3 earthquake, later revised to 5.9, struck 40km north of Te Anau at 9.14pm on Thursday 16 July at a depth of 51km. NEMA issued a tsunami warning, downgraded it to an advisory, then cancelled it once no tsunami signal appeared. GeoNet logged around ten aftershocks between magnitude 3.3 and 4.3 in the hours after. The Netflix cast, in the region for the shoot above, felt it firsthand.

For the visitor economy the headline is what did not happen. There were no official reports of damage, the roads stayed open and the Milford Road, the single artery to Milford Sound, kept operating normally through the peak of the winter season. Fiordland sits on one of the country's most active fault systems, so operators there plan for this, but a clean recovery in the middle of July is still worth noting when the alternative is closed roads and cancelled cruises at the worst possible time.

## Queenstown Airport is planning for its busiest winter yet

Queenstown Airport expects to move more than 785,000 passengers between July and September, up 6.8% on the 735,344 it handled last winter. The airport is planning for peak days near 10,000 passengers and around 70 aircraft movements, and points to new direct Brisbane services from Air New Zealand and Jetstar, the July school holidays and stronger Chinese demand via the Australian visa waiver as the drivers.

The strain shows up on the ground rather than in the terminal. As yesterday's roadworks story underlined, the constraint on a record winter is no longer air capacity but the beds, rental fleets and roads that have to absorb the arrivals once they land. Destination Queenstown chief executive Mat Woods calls the district "the engine room for New Zealand tourism", which is true of the revenue and also of the pressure.

## Tourism's national gathering sets its 2026 theme

Tourism Industry Aotearoa has confirmed the theme for this year's Tourism Summit Aotearoa: "Tourism Inc: Distinctive by Design". The framing asks operators and the sector as a whole to compete on what is genuinely and uniquely New Zealand rather than on price or volume, which is a pointed message in a market where AI-flattened recommendations reward the already-famous. The keynote goes to George Cao, co-founder of Dragon Trail International, whose read on the China market is worth the ticket on its own.

The summit and the New Zealand Tourism Awards run on Wednesday 4 November at Te Pae Christchurch. Early-bird registration closes on 30 July, so the decision point for anyone weighing attendance is now rather than October.
